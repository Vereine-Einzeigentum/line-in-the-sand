# LINE IN THE SAND — Setting & Stylistic Energy

The story lives inside Saudi Arabia's THE LINE — the 170-kilometer mirrored linear city that was completed seventy-plus years ago and has been inhabited continuously ever since. It is not a new megaproject. It is an old city now, layered and worn smooth, with four generations of people walking its corridors simultaneously.

Simultaneously, because four generations ago the great-grandparents of the present residents signed a contract with a Chinese-Arab cloning company — 淼رسومالعهود, *Miǎo Rusum al-Uhud*, The Boundless Fees of the Covenants. The terms were generous on paper: housing inside THE LINE, medical care, body restoration on death, all in perpetuity. The terms bound not only the signatories but every descendant after them, forever. The contract did not have an exit clause. And the signatories themselves never died out. They are still here. The same great-grandparents who signed seventy years ago walk the same corridors as their great-grandchildren, brought back from the vats every time the body fails, each restoration a little further off from the last one. The originals are the most degraded — they've been brought back the most times. They sit in the back of the noodle stalls remembering things the way they don't quite remember them anymore. Their great-grandchildren bring them tea.

The population does not turn over. It accretes. Every era's people are still present. The arcades that opened in the 2030s are still there because the people who installed them are still there, and so are their kids and their kids' kids. Cultural layers are not memories; they are people sitting next to each other. When a person dies, the body comes back. The one who comes back is not exactly the one who left. *Help kms* returns "If only it were that simple..." The chain does not break with the body.

The world around THE LINE has moved on. The West receded long ago — not collapsed, just stopped being the gravity well. The pull is in the Gulf, China, South Asia, Iran, Russia. The Saud royals are untouchable but distant. The police are underpaid and depend on district taxes. The Triads run trade. The Boundless owns the floor under everyone's feet, but you never see them, the way you never see the deed to your house. None of this is treated as exotic by the people inside the story. It's just the world. It's been the world for a long time.

Culturally, everyone is here, and has been for four generations of living people. Hijab next to tech-goth next to anthropomorphic deer in a band jacket. Arabic and Mandarin and Urdu and Russian on the signage, sometimes all on the same sign. Mosques and arcades and zine racks and noodle stalls and the smell of solder. The characters in the story are mostly the people who keep this running: fixers, fences, scrappers, hackers, medics, musicians. Working-class heirs to a covenant they did not sign. The story is about the people in the vents.

The visual language is 3D lowpoly models rendered, then pixelated, then scanlined. Faceted geometry, visible polygons, hard-edged shadows, a CRT overlay sitting on top of the whole thing like you're watching it through old glass. This is the right register for a world where the future arrived seventy-plus years ago and got old without permission. Lowpoly is the look of an era that promised slickness and didn't deliver, and the technology in THE LINE matches: nobody is buying new goggles, new servers, new fluorescents. They are using what their parents used, what their grandparents used, what their great-grandparents installed when this was new. The faceted polygons aren't a stylistic flourish — they're what things actually look like when nothing has been resurfaced for decades.

The baseline runs through every frame and is narrow: deep purple-black ambient, cold and unloving, sitting under and behind everything; warm amber light from below on anything alive, from a bulb or a screen or a streetlamp or a fire in an alley; teal as the cool counter-accent on hair and glass and certain plastics; a single small red LED somewhere on any piece of working tech. That's the constant. Everything else is per-district.

**The safehouse** is the smallest palette: one warm bulb against deep purple shadow and almost nothing else. Maybe a cyan-glow from a tablet across the room. Restraint. The single amber source is the entire emotional argument.

**The scrap zone** is industrial decay: sodium-orange streetlamps (the specific 1980s loading-dock orange, not amber), rust and oxidation on every metal surface, fluorescent cyan-green coolant pooled in low spots, dust the color of khaki and old paper. Chemical and washed. No neon.

**The fence shop** is fluorescent flicker: cool cyan-white from a bad bulb, warm yellow under the counter where the actual work happens, faded posters in cream and rust and sand, the fence themself in deep brown or navy absorbing all the bad lighting evenly. Worn cloth, stained linoleum, the patient smell of solder.

**The mall and the souk** are warm, busy, multicultural commerce: incandescent gold-yellow overhead, varied saturated signage in cyan, gold, green, red, blue, with red lanterns at the food court and brass-and-gold lettering on the storefronts. The floor is starry-dark purple. The mall is loud with color but it is *warm* loud, not neon loud. This is the daytime palette of public civic life across the Gulf and East Asia — warm incandescent, gold, saturated paint and cloth and food coloring. It is not pink and it is not cyberpunk. It is a working-class commercial space across four generations and it looks like one.

**Bars and clubs** are the only place magenta lives. Pink and hot-magenta neon, ultraviolet, the specific saturated chroma of nightlife. The mall might have a club tucked into the corner of the food court and that's where the magenta is. Outside the door it's incandescent and starry-purple again. Magenta is a venue color, not an ambient one.

**The mosque and the shrine spaces** are visual rest. Cream walls, lapis blue tile, gold leaf calligraphy, brass lamps, deep carpet red. The ambient is warm cream and cool lapis. Light comes from everywhere, soft. The geometry here is also a little smoother — these surfaces are maintained because someone loves them.

**The server farm and utility levels** are the building's nervous system. Deep cool purple ambient, red LED rows on rack faces (warm point-sources, the *alive* indicators), cyan LED rows (cool point-sources, the *thinking* indicators), pale gold flicker from a monitor running an old terminal, bundles of cable in saturated vinyl primary colors — yellow, blue, red, black sheathing — the only saturated color in the frame. Cold and humming.

**The Boundless** has its own brand and the artist needs to recognize it on sight. The company's visual identity is precise and consistent across every space and document they own:

- **Cream background.** Paper-cream, off-white-warm, the color of an old letter. This is the dominant wall and surface color in any Boundless space.
- **Lacquer black** for trim, fittings, architectural frames, calligraphy. Deep glossy ink-finish black. The defining accent material.
- **Arabic calligraphy in lacquer black, with a golden drop-shadow.** Formal, traditional, prestige-loaded. Every official sign and document.
- **The logo: 淼.** The Chinese character for "boundless waters" — three 水 radicals stacked in a triangle — rendered in deep red, inside a lacquer-black circle. This is the corporate chop, the seal, the *yin*. It appears on contracts, on doors, on equipment, on uniforms, on every surface they want to claim. Once you can recognize the red 淼 in the black circle, you can see the company everywhere.
- **Mosque blue geometric fractal** in the background — the Islamic geometric tile pattern, rendered in deep ceramic blue, used architecturally behind the calligraphy or as ornamental background. The Chinese-Arab synthesis made structural.
- **Gold** as the prestige accent — calligraphy drop-shadow, brass fittings, fine inlay. Sparingly. Gold is for the documents and the doorways.

When the artist needs to indicate a space belongs to the Boundless — a clone vat, a contract office, a maintenance bay they own — this is the language: cream + lacquer black + mosque-blue fractal + the red 淼 chop somewhere in frame + gold accents. Beautiful, ancient-feeling, Chinese-imperial-meets-Arab-imperial, unmistakably *theirs*. The geometry in their spaces gets a little smoother because they actually maintain things. The amber under-light is absent here. Light comes from above and from inside the walls. This is what being owned looks like, and it is beautiful, and that is the point.

**The exterior** — the desert outside THE LINE — is sand and bone and dust. Sodium streetlamps in the far distance. The mirrored wall of the city reflecting deep purple-black sky back at itself. The mirror is the only smooth surface outside, and even the mirror is dust-streaked at human height. Vast and unloving in a different way than the building is unloving. The building doesn't love anyone because it's owned. The desert doesn't love anyone because it never did.

The lowpoly carries all of this without softening it. Faceted geometry handles material differentiation by surface treatment: brushed steel and dirty aluminum on infrastructure, anodized colored metal on old equipment, concrete on every structural wall, faded sun-bleached plastic on signage and furniture, cloth that has been worn for years and carries dust in the weave, polished stone only in mosques and Boundless interiors, glass wiped a million times and slightly cloudy and slightly scratched, lacquer black where the Boundless lives, cream where they wall you in. Cables everywhere — the visible nervous system of a building nobody maintains to spec.

The visible polygons are honesty. Nothing pretends to be smooth except the surfaces owned by the entity that owns you. The world is constructed and admits it. The CRT scanlines are a generation marker — the equipment is inherited, the eyes are aged, the bodies have been restored a few too many times. Nothing is HD because nothing has been HD in living memory. The lighting is heat against cold: amber from below on the living things, cool deep purple from above on the building, district-specific color in between. The Boundless lights from above and from inside the walls. Life lights from below.

The artist's job is to make all of that feel inevitable — to make each space instantly recognizable by palette without breaking the lowpoly+scanline+amber-underlight baseline, to make the mall feel warm and the club feel loud and the safehouse feel small and the scrap zone feel chemical and the Boundless feel beautiful and wrong, and to make every frame look like something that has been this way for a long time, will stay this way, and is somehow, against the terms of the contract, alive.
