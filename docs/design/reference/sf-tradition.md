# Non-Western SF — The Actual Reading Traditions

The Western literary establishment filters non-Western SF through what becomes legible to Western readers — what got translated by Ken Liu, what won Hugo or Booker, what Tor or Wesleyan or Penguin India picked up. Those authors are real and good but they are not what the actual readership of these regions has been reading for the past forty to sixty years.

What follows is the pulp, the serialized magazine, the pocket novel, the web platform, the kiosk paperback. The forms most readers in these regions actually engaged with. None of these are in the Anglophone canon. All of them shaped the imaginations of tens of millions of people who are the demographic LINE IN THE SAND's characters are descended from.

## Egypt and the Arab World — Rewayat Pocket Novels

The Modern Arab Association (المؤسسة العربية الحديثة), based in Egypt and led by publisher Hamdy Mostafa, ran a cultural project from the mid-1980s called **Rewayāt Masreyya Lel Gēb (روايات مصرية للجيب — Egyptian Pocket Novels)**. Cheap, small-format paperbacks sold at kiosks across the Arab world, designed for adolescents and young adults, read discreetly during boring classes and on long bus rides. Two writers defined the project:

**Nabil Farouk (نبيل فاروق, 1956-2020)** — Tanta-born physician turned full-time novelist. Wrote 500+ novels. Sold millions of copies across the Arab world from the mid-80s onward. Two major series:
- **Malaf Al Mostakbal (ملف المستقبل — The Future File)** — 160 volumes, 1984-2009 — explicit science fiction series following an Egyptian scientific intelligence team led by Nour El-Din Mahmoud. Titles include *Death Rays*, *Disappearance of a Rocket*, *The City of the Depths*, *The Aliens*, *The Mysterious Bomb*, *A Visitor from the Future*, *Fight of the Senses*, *The Deadly Shaking*
- **Ragol Al Mostaheel (رجل المستحيل — The Man of the Impossible)** — 160 volumes, 1984-2009 — spy fiction with SF elements, Egyptian intelligence officer Adham Sabry vs. Mossad/CIA/KGB
- Other series: *Cocktail 2000*, *Andalucía's Knight*, *Flowers*, *Zoom*, *Roaa*

**Ahmed Khaled Tawfik (أحمد خالد توفيق, 1962-2018)** — Tanta physician (overlapping generation/place with Farouk), considered the "father of Arabic horror." Major series in the Rewayat line:
- **Ma Waraa Al Tabiaa (ما وراء الطبيعة — Beyond Nature)** — paranormal/horror series, 81 volumes 1993-2014, protagonist Refaat Ismail
- **Safari** — supernatural-medical-thriller series
- **Fantazia** — alternate-reality / parallel-dimension series
- Also: *Utopia* (2008) — a standalone dystopian novel about a gated wealthy enclave in 2023 Egypt while the masses outside survive on synthetic meat. This one is famous in the Arab world; ignored in the West until very recently.

**Yatakhayaloon** (يتخيلون — "They Imagine") — Saudi/Arab SF collective founded around 2010 by Yasser Bahjat and Ibraheem Abbas, who self-published their novel *HWJAN* when no publisher would take it. Yatakhayaloon also publishes anthologies and runs SF fan communities online across the Gulf.

Worth understanding: when a forty-something Egyptian or Saudi or Yemeni or Iraqi character in THE LINE is mentioned reading something for fun, they were raised on this tradition. Their parents and grandparents traded Rewayat paperbacks in school. The covers are bold primary colors, the typography is dense Arabic calligraphy with English-genre callouts, the format is pocket-sized for back-pocket carrying. The grandkids' equivalent in 2096 is a phone app distributing the same kind of stories.

## China — Science Fiction World Magazine and Qidian

**Science Fiction World (科幻世界, SFW)** — founded 1979 in Chengdu, Sichuan. Monthly. Current circulation 130,000; peak after 1999 hit 300,000-400,000 per issue. THE central magazine for Chinese SF readership for nearly five decades. The Galaxy Awards (银河奖), founded 1985 and given through SFW, are the major Chinese SF awards — the "Big Four" of Chinese SF all came up through this magazine and its readership.

The Chinese-canonical "Big Four" — **Liu Cixin (刘慈欣), Han Song (韩松), He Xi (何夕), and Wang Jinkang (王晋康)**. Liu Cixin became the global brand through Ken Liu's translation. The other three remain largely unavailable in English:
- **Han Song** — journalist for Xinhua, also one of China's most respected SF writers. *Subway* (地铁), *High-Speed Rail* (高铁), *Hospital* trilogy. Surrealist, political, deeply pessimistic — the opposite of Liu Cixin's hard-SF optimism
- **Wang Jinkang** — chemical engineer from Henan. Hugely prolific. Themes around cloning, genetic engineering, bioethics. Massive Chinese readership; virtually unknown in English
- **He Xi** — engineer, *Six Paths of Reincarnation* (六道众生), philosophical/cosmological hard SF

**Qidian (起点中文网)** — founded 2002, Tencent-owned, the largest Chinese web fiction platform. VIP membership charges roughly 2 fen (¢2) per 1000 words. Top authors make hundreds of millions of yuan. The longest-running sci-fi web novel on Qidian is 41 million characters and still updating daily since 2017. Web fiction is the actual format most Chinese SF readers under 35 consume — read on phone, daily updates, very long form. Top earners:
- **Tang Jia San Shao (唐家三少)** — fantasy/SF blends, *Douluo Dalu* series
- **Tian Can Tu Dou (天蚕土豆)** — xianxia, *Battle Through the Heavens*
- **I Eat Tomatoes (我吃西红柿)** — *Coiling Dragon*
- **Mao Ni (猫腻)** — *Joy of Life*, *Spies* — combines cultivation with futuristic sci-fi

**Jinjiang (晋江文学城, JJWXC)** — female-oriented web fiction platform. Major writers MXTX (Mò Xiāng Tóng Xiù, 墨香铜臭) and Priest. Heavy on danmei (boys-love) but extensive SF and dystopia categories.

Specific Chinese genre vocabulary: **xianxia (仙侠)** — Daoist cultivation/immortality, **xuanhuan (玄幻)** — Eastern fantasy with magic systems, **wuxia (武侠)** — martial arts heroes, **kehuan (科幻)** — science fiction proper. Most actually-popular Chinese web novels blend these — a story might be 90% xianxia immortal cultivation but with starships and alien races. This blending is unfamiliar to Western readers but standard to Chinese ones.

## India — Kalpavigyan (Bengali)

**Adrish Bardhan (অদ্রীশ বর্ধন, 1932-2019)** — Kolkata writer/editor who single-handedly built the Bengali SF genre. Coined the term **kalpavigyan (কল্পবিজ্ঞান, "imagined-science")** in 1962. Founded the first Bengali SF magazine **Ashchorjo (আশ্চর্য — "Wonder")** in January 1963. Magazine ran 1963-72, then his second magazine *Fantastic* from 1975, then *Kalpabiswa* in the mid-1980s. He also founded Alpha Beta Publication, ran the Science Fiction Cine Club of Kolkata (first in Asia), and produced SF radio dramas on All India Radio.

Bardhan's stature in Bengali SF is comparable to John W. Campbell's in American SF — a single editor who shaped a generation of writers.

**Premendra Mitra (1904-1988)** — *Ghanada (ঘনাদা)* series, started 1945. Ghan Shyam Das ("Ghanada") is an old adventurer in a Kolkata boarding house who tells improbable scientific tall tales to younger lodgers. 100+ stories. Foundational pulp.

**Hemendra Kumar Ray (1888-1963)** — Bengali pulp adventure-SF, *Amanushik Manush* (Superhuman Humans), psychic powers, lost civilizations.

**Satyajit Ray (1921-1992)** — *Professor Shonku (প্রোফেসর শঙ্কু)* series, 38 stories, 1965-1992. Bengali scientist-adventurer who travels the world and beyond. Massively popular in Bengali; some translations exist but the readership is overwhelmingly in Bengali.

**Kalpabiswa Publications** — current Bengali SF publisher in Kolkata. Magazine launched online 2016, physical anthologies since 2018. Contributors include Bardhan (until his death), Ranen Ghosh, Yashodhara Ray Chaudhuri, Anish Deb, Amitananda Das. They publish in Bengali; almost nothing translated.

## India — Hindi Pulp (Meerut Tradition)

The Hindi pulp publishing industry was centered in **Meerut, Uttar Pradesh** from the 1960s onward — paperback novels sold at railway station kiosks for ₹50-100, read on trains, traded among friends, with a print and readership scale that makes Tor Books look like a niche operation.

**Ved Prakash Sharma (1955-2017)** — Meerut. Wrote 176 novels (ghostwrote 23). His 1992 novel *Vardi Wala Gunda* sold **15 lakh (1.5 million) copies on its first day of release**. Lifetime sales ~80 million copies. Predominantly detective/thriller with SF elements seeping in. Main characters: Vijay, Vikas, Raghunath, Alphanse, Vibha Jindal, Keshav Pandit, Raina. Also wrote Bollywood screenplays.

**Surender Mohan Pathak ("SMP", b. 1940)** — Delhi. Still active at 85. Bestselling Hindi crime fiction writer. 4-5 novels/year for 55+ years. Each new release sells 25,000+ copies within six months. Two major series:
- **Sunil Chakravarty series** — 100 novels, investigative journalist protagonist
- **Vimal series** — *Sardar Surendra Singh Sohal*, "a gangster with a conscience"

**Ved Prakash Kamboj** — 1960s-80s Meerut pulp horror/thriller, *Preton ka Nirmata* (The Maker of Ghosts), *Railgadi Ka Bhoot* (The Ghost of the Train).

**Om Prakash Sharma** — 1960s, one of the three foundational Hindi pulp writers.

**Ibn-e-Safi (Asrar Ahmad, 1928-1980)** — Urdu/Hindi, wrote in both India and Pakistan. *Jasoosi Dunya (جاسوسی دنیا — Spy World)* series 1952-79, 125 books. Detective Imran with sci-fi elements (mind-control devices, gadgets, mysterious villains). Foundational influence on all subsequent South Asian thriller/SF writing.

Hindi pulp is mostly crime/spy/thriller with SF elements rather than pure SF, but the format and reading culture is the same as Egyptian Rewayat — kiosk paperback, mass-market, vast circulation, no Western canonization.

## India — Tamil (Sujatha and Vikatan)

**Sujatha (S. Rangarajan, 1935-2008)** — wrote under his wife's name. Electronics engineer at Bharat Electronics Limited (Bangalore) — supervised the design and production of India's electronic voting machine still in use today. Wrote hundreds of short stories, dozens of novels, weekly columns, screenplays. The towering figure of modern Tamil SF and popular literature.

Sujatha's signature work: **En Iniya Iyandhira (என் இனிய இயந்திரா — My Beloved Robot/My Sweet Machine)** — serialized in *Ananda Vikatan* in the 1980s, set in a dystopian 2021 with the Indian subcontinent under autocracy, female protagonist Nila, robots, automation, child-control regimes. Followed by sequel *Meendum Jeeno*.

He also serialized SF in **Kumudam, Kalki, Kungumam, Dinamani Kadhir** — the major Tamil weeklies. His column **Yen Yedharku Yeppadi (Why What-for How)** in Junior Vikatan was a science-FAQ that taught a generation of Tamil readers about computing, physics, biology, and engineering in everyday Tamil. **Silicon Chip** column in Dinamani Kadhir introduced Tamil readers to early computing.

Earlier Tamil SF pioneers: **Kalki (R. Krishnamurthy, 1899-1954)** — *Mohini Theevu*. **Pudumaipithan (1906-1948)** — *Kadavul* and short SF.

The Tamil tradition is serialized-in-magazines first, books later. Most Tamil readers encountered Sujatha through Ananda Vikatan's weekly installments, not as standalone books.

## Iran — Fantasy Academy and Untranslated Persian SF

Iranian SF has had a difficult publishing history under censorship. Most Persian-language SF is unavailable in English.

**'Abdalhusayn San'atīzādah** — *Rustam dar qarn-i bīst-u duvvum* (رستم در قرن بیست و دوم — *Rustam in the 22nd Century*, 1934). A scientist named Jancas in 22nd-century Sistan-Baluchistan invents a machine that reanimates the dead body and soul of Rustam, his horse Rakhsh, and his sidekick Zangiano from the *Shahnameh*. One of the foundational works of Persian SF. Never translated.

**Fantasy Academy (فانتزی)** at fantasy.ir — the largest Iranian SF/fantasy online community since the 2000s. Persian-language; translates short fiction in both directions. Runs an annual short story contest and award. Discussion forums, fan fiction, original work.

**Zoha Kazemi** — contemporary Iranian SF writer. *Humanoid* (2022) — stem-cell immortality, characters choose new professions, genders, and names every 40 years. Translated to English; published in Iran first.

**Sadegh Hedayat (1903-1951)** — *The Blind Owl (Boof-e Koor)* — surreal, proto-SF, dream-state Tehran.

The Travelers (مسافران, *Musāfirān*) — 70-episode Iranian TV series, 2009. Alien family adapting to Tehran life, sitcom format. Massive in Iran; unknown elsewhere.

## What This Means for LINE IN THE SAND

A 70-year-old grandmother in THE LINE in 2096 was a child in 2030. The reading tradition she grew up with — and that her parents traded among each other a generation earlier — was not the Penguin Modern Classics shelf or the Hugo Award winners. It was Nabil Farouk pocket novels traded in Cairo schoolyards, or Ananda Vikatan serialized installments her mother saved up to buy, or Bardhan's *Ashchorjo* back issues at the College Street book stalls in Kolkata, or *Science Fiction World* magazines stacked on a Chengdu bedside table, or *Vardi Wala Gunda* read on a Meerut commuter train. In 2096, her grandchildren on THE LINE are reading the descendants of those forms — phone-app serialized chapters, kiosk-equivalent micropayment installments, web-novel longform — distributed through whatever the Boundless's content infrastructure permits, with the same compressed, fast-moving, pulp-DNA storytelling.

The genre vocabulary they use is not "cyberpunk" and "space opera" — those are Western categories. The vocabulary is **kalpavigyan, kehuan, xianxia, خيال علمي (khayāl 'ilmī, Arabic science-fiction), Rewayat-style, Vikatan-serial, Qidian-format**. The vocabulary is the format: the *pocket novel*, the *weekly column*, the *daily web update*, the *anthology magazine*, the *fortnightly serial*. The vocabulary is the kiosk, the railway station, the magazine wall, the WeChat reader app.

The aesthetic these traditions share, and that THE LINE inherits:
- **Serialized, episodic, never quite resolving** — readers come back next week for the next installment
- **Mixed-genre by default** — a single Sujatha novel has hard SF + political thriller + family drama + romance + comic relief. Western "literary purity" of genre is not the standard
- **Mass-market unashamed** — no apology for being pulp. The point is being read by millions, not by a curated few
- **Vernacular language** — Bengali, Tamil, Hindi, Arabic, Mandarin, Persian. Not translated upward into English literary register
- **Format-native** — written for the magazine page, the kiosk paperback, the phone screen. Not for hardcover-shelf eternity
- **Bodies and bureaucracies, not concepts** — characters live in apartments, work jobs, eat food, fight neighbors, deal with cops. The SF element is layered on top of real material life, not abstracted from it
- **Long sequences, not standalone novels** — Farouk wrote 320 pocket novels in two series. Pathak wrote 100 in one series. Web novels run to tens of millions of characters. Series-thinking is default

When LINE IN THE SAND draws on "SF tradition," this is the tradition. Not what won the Hugo. What got read at the kiosk.

The artist should know: a poster or magazine cover in the world of THE LINE looks more like a 1990s Nabil Farouk paperback or a 1980s Ananda Vikatan front page than like a *Wired* magazine cover or a Mondo movie poster. Dense calligraphy, lurid color, bold contrast, multilingual headlines, a protagonist's face mid-action, a kiosk-friendly small format. That's the visual DNA.
