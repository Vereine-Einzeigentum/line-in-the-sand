# Urban Life Research — Eight Cities, June 2026

Field research toward grounding LINE IN THE SAND in real contemporary urban texture instead of cyberpunk-default or gentrified-default tropes. Each city is observed in two layers — the photogenic gentrified zone everyone shows, and the working-class ground truth where most of the population actually lives.

The mistake to avoid is doubling: not just neon-magenta cyberpunk romanticism, but also the curated specialty-coffee minimalism that has become the second cliché of "the global city." THE LINE is neither. Its population isn't customers of either world. They live in the third texture — the dense, working, poor, multilingual, sweating, sleeping-on-fabric, washing-on-the-balcony, headphones-blasting-over-the-machines reality that runs every megacity in the world right now.

---

## Riyadh

**Gentrified zone.** Saudi Arabia has 5,130 branded coffee shops — the largest market in the Middle East — and they're how a young population (median age ~29) socializes, works, and stays out late. Riyadh's nightlife is restaurants, lounges, gaming arenas, comedy clubs, immersive cultural attractions, and late-night patisseries that stay open past midnight. There are no nightclubs in the alcohol sense; there are events, halls, and pop-ups where you might dance to a global DJ at Qiddiya, but the venue is dry and the patrons are families and singles mixed. Esports is enormous — Riyadh hosts the Esports World Cup with prize pools in the millions. Comedy in Arabic is huge. Concerts happen openly now after Vision 2030. The architectural texture is split: brutalist mid-century concrete from oil-boom decades layered with new Vision 2030 megaprojects (Boulevard City, Diriyah, Qiddiya) layered onto original Najdi mudbrick villages.

**Working life.** Southern Riyadh is the migrant city. Manfouha was a Najdi village swallowed by the 1970s expansion, then abandoned by Saudis as they moved to newer northern neighborhoods — now it's the dense Ethiopian, Sudanese, Yemeni, Egyptian district, with Cairo-style old-alleyway texture. Ghubaira nearby is the Sudanese neighborhood — charcoal-grilled meat smell, Sudanese music from electronics shops, coffee rituals with cardamom, elderly men laughing outside restaurants. Al-Khalidiah is the Indian/Pakistani/Bangladeshi/Yemeni quarter. Al-Batha is the old central district where 1.5+ million expatriates work and live, the visible center of the migrant economy. The Saudi government periodically raids these neighborhoods — 2013 saw mass deportations of 163,000+ Ethiopians, with violent attacks on workers in Manfouha that killed several. The kafala (sponsorship) system has been reformed in name but workers can still be made undocumented by their employer canceling their visa. These neighborhoods host the cheapest restaurants in the city, the most layered street life, the most actual pluralism — and remain socially invisible in the Vision 2030 image.

## Baghdad

**Gentrified zone.** Baghdad is rebuilding itself culturally after two decades of war, and the rebuild is being done by people in their twenties and thirties who grew up inside the bombings and decided to make a life anyway. Al-Mutanabbi Street — the book market named after a 10th-century poet — was renovated by private banks at the end of the 2020s and is now the gathering point for the cultural scene. Karrada district is where young people meet at colorful chairs on the sidewalk, drink tea and coffee, and smoke. Mansour district is upmarket. Streetwear brands like Zuqaq13 use Arabic calligraphy in graphic-design ways, reclaiming the language from the militants who damaged it. The texture is concrete T-walls painted with murals, solar-powered streetlights (post-conflict infrastructure), coffeehouses with deep traditions, the smell of masgouf cooked over open fires along the Tigris. Military fatigues are casual fashion for young women.

**Working life.** Sadr City — the vast Shia slum on Baghdad's eastern edge — is home to 2-3 million people, about a quarter of greater Baghdad's population. It was built by Saddam in the 1950s-60s as housing for rural Shia migrants from the south, named Saddam City, then renamed after the cleric Mohammed al-Sadr who was killed in 1999. It was the staging ground for the Mahdi Army through the 2000s and the site of some of the worst sectarian bombings in Iraqi history. The texture in 2026: dense low-rise concrete housing, overflowing trash bins, wild goats let off by their owners to scavenge because feed costs too much, sewage problems where leaks come into houses, kids playing barefoot soccer on dirt lots, women going into cosmetics shops because life continues, girls in Islamic schools, poultry merchants playing music while they sell, orphan rehabilitation centers feeding 330+ children whose parents were killed in suicide attacks, posters of Imam Hussein on every wall. Hassoun the blind man in his small Al-Fdhiliya house with his family watching television. The neighborhood does its own governance through Sadrist clerics — formal Iraqi police are sparse here. People are poor, and the city continues.

## Gujarat (Ahmedabad, Surat)

**Gentrified zone.** Gujarat is one of the engines of the multipolar economy. Surat cuts 90% of the world's diamonds, runs the textile capital of India, and is the fastest-growing city in the world by some forecasts (2019-2035). Ahmedabad has a metro population approaching 10 million, the historic walled old city (Pol architecture — courtyard houses inside courtyard blocks, accessible by narrow secret alleys), and the new Sabarmati Riverfront development. The streets are full of two-wheelers, auto-rickshaws, mango carts, garment dyers with rainbow hands, diamond couriers carrying briefcases that could buy a house. Religion is dense — Hindu, Jain, Muslim, Parsi, all visible. Vegetarianism is the default. Garba and dandiya during Navratri turn entire cities into outdoor dance halls for nine nights.

**Working life.** Surat has 2 million textile workers in 50,000 manufacturing units, running 700,000+ power-looms that produce 40% of India's synthetic fabric, 45 million square metres of raw cloth per day. Workers are predominantly Odia migrants from Ganjam district and other interior states, plus a heavy Muslim labor force. Shifts run 7am-7pm or 9pm-9am — 12 hours each, around the clock. Wages: ₹400-600 per shift, roughly $5-7. A 2020 audiometry study found 94% of power-loom workers have hearing loss; workers use headphones blasting music to drown out the machines, which causes more damage. Lungs full of fine silica dust and carbon particles. Class 8 dropouts dying within 24 hours of starting work from electrocution on poorly maintained machines. Pandesara Industrial Area (ten kilometers from Surat railway station) houses workers in 8'×8' rooms shared between two or four men. Workers sleep on piles of fabric in the factories during break. Factories are deliberately registered under the Shops and Establishments Act instead of the Factories Act to avoid having to provide protections. The aesthetic of public life is bright color, embroidery, mirror-work, gold thread — but the bodies producing it are deafening themselves and breathing dust 12 hours a day in cramped fluorescent-lit halls.

## Singapore

**Gentrified zone.** Hawker culture is the structural answer to Singapore's question of how a multi-ethnic city eats together. UNESCO-recognized in 2020. ~100 hawker centres across the city, each a "community dining room" where Chinese, Malay, Indian, and Peranakan dishes are served from adjacent stalls. The drink-ordering language alone is its own register — kopi (coffee), teh (tea), with permutation suffixes (kopi-O, kopi-C, kopi-gao, kopi-peng, teh tarik pulled from a great height) and labeled cups. The architectural texture is HDB blocks — public housing slabs in pastel colors, organized into precincts each with its own hawker centre and wet market. The MRT runs constantly. English, Mandarin, Malay, and Tamil all share signage by law. Internet is fast, cashless payment is universal. The new hawker centres (13 planned by 2027) come with solar panels and pneumatic waste systems.

**Working life.** Singapore has 280,000+ migrant construction workers, mostly from Bangladesh, India, and southern China. They build the city — the skyscrapers, the malls, the new hawker centres — for $400-500/month. They live in purpose-built dormitories at the western edges of the island (Tuas, Woodlands, Sungei Tengah) — Westlite, Avery, the named dormitory operators. 12 workers per room is standard. Bunk beds stacked, shared cooking area, shared bathroom, fluorescent lighting, washing line on every available rail. The 2020 COVID-19 outbreak ripped through these dormitories and made visible what was always there: a parallel society sealed off from the official city, where workers had no agency over their own movement and the Singapore government quarantined entire compounds for months. The Foreign Employee Dormitories Act (FEDA, 2015) regulates standards, but workers are still confined to dormitories and worksites for the duration of their contracts. Their only public space is Sunday at Little India, where tens of thousands of South Asian men gather along Serangoon Road for the one day they're not on a worksite — phone calls home, money transfers, plates of biriyani, barber visits, the loud collective joy of being briefly outside the dormitory. The Singapore the official city shows to tourists is built and serviced by people the official city tries to keep invisible the other six days of the week.

## Hong Kong

**Gentrified zone.** Hong Kong is layered vertical density — apartment blocks 50 stories tall, MTR underneath, malls in the basement, neon signage (or, since 2020, increasingly LED-replaced) at street level. Cha chaan teng (茶餐廳, "tea restaurant") is the everyday cafe-diner: yuanyang (coffee+tea), milk tea, pineapple buns, macaroni soup with ham, plastic chairs, fluorescent lighting, shared tables, fast turnover. Temple Street Night Market in Yau Ma Tei is what remains of the "Poor Man's Night Club" tradition — fortune tellers (palm, face, bird), amateur Cantonese opera near Tin Hau Temple, clay-pot rice at dai pai dong stalls. Mong Kok is sneakerhead culture, Fa Yuen Street for fashion, Sham Shui Po for electronics scrap and rebuilds. The Bird Garden is elderly Cantonese men bringing songbirds to socialize.

**Working life.** 220,000+ Hongkongers live in subdivided flats — 110,000 total units, most in dilapidated tenement buildings in Sham Shui Po, Kowloon City, and Yau Tsim Mong. A typical unit is 50 square feet, made by slicing one apartment into 12 partitioned cells with thin wooden boards or wire mesh. Single sink, bathroom with no door, no kitchen. Rent: HK$2,000-4,000/month (US$250-500). The most extreme version is the "cage home" or "coffin home" — boxlike compartments stacked from floor to ceiling, 4×4×6 ft, accessed by climbing onto a ladder. A 68-year-old factory worker shares an unlicensed bedspace flat with 18 other men and women in Mong Kok, returns from his food-factory night shift, climbs into his box, sleeps. Three-generation households squeeze into 200-square-foot subdivided flats. Hong Kong has the highest urban density in the world; the gap between the typical apartment in mid-levels (a single luxury unit can be 3,000 sq ft) and a coffin home in Sham Shui Po is one of the most extreme intra-city wealth divergences anywhere. The 2024 regulations attempting to set an 86 sq ft minimum were called too small by 60% of tenants. This is what's behind the cha chaan teng and the dai pai dong and the bright Temple Street neon — a population that lives in containers stacked on each other and works fourteen-hour shifts to afford the container.

## Beijing

**Gentrified zone.** Beijing's hutongs — narrow alleys of single-story courtyard houses around Drum Tower, Lama Temple, and Wudaoying — have been substantially repurposed. Old courtyards are now wabi-sabi-style coffee shops, indie boutiques, Spanish tapas bars, Mexican kitchens, ramen counters, rooftop cocktail bars. A 600-year-old temple in Dongcheng has been converted into a minimalist specialty coffee space. The vibe is "Chinese-Japanese-Western minimalist coffee culture" — Australian-trained baristas, single-origin beans, exposed brick. Outside the hutong zone, Beijing is enormous concrete ring roads, six subway lines deep, surveillance cameras every few meters, WeChat for everything. Cars are mostly electric now; air is much cleaner than a decade ago.

**Working life.** Beijing's migrant workforce — somewhere between 8 and 10 million people without Beijing hukou (household registration) — lives in chengzhongcun (城中村, "villages in the city"), informal settlements on the urban-rural fringe. Most of the inner-city chengzhongcun were demolished through the 2010s. The remaining ones are at the outer rings — Yellow Field, one of the largest, houses 50,000 low-paid migrants working as street vendors, janitors, caterers, construction laborers, food-delivery riders. The texture: towering skyscrapers visible from the edges of paved roads that turn into rough dirt roads leading into the village. Dirt alleys lined with self-built, high-density rental units of 2-8 stories. Garbage piled in small heaps in the alleys. Hukou exclusion means these workers' children can't attend Beijing public schools, can't access Beijing healthcare without enormous fees, can't legally settle. They live in Beijing, work in Beijing, build Beijing, and remain administratively rural. The 2017 "low-end population" expulsions cleared many chengzhongcun in a single brutal winter. The hutong cafes drink their coffee on streets the migrant workers can't legally live on.

## Dubai

**Gentrified zone.** Dubai Mall has an aquarium, an ice rink, a ski slope, a souk reproduction, the world's longest indoor canal. Burj Khalifa, Palm Jumeirah, the Marina, the world-largest-of-everything. Glass towers, marble lobbies, six-star hotels, gold souks. Emirati luxury as global brand.

**Working life.** The visible city is built and maintained by 50,000+ migrant workers in Sonapur — labor camps half an hour into the desert — and dozens of similar settlements. Workers are mostly from Pakistan, India, Bangladesh, Nepal, the Philippines. 10-hour days, six days a week. The kafala system has been reformed in name — but passport confiscation, wage theft, and the criminalization of "absconding" still produce a class of stranded workers fed by charity organizations like Karama Kanteen. Karama, Deira, and Bur Dubai are the central neighborhoods where migrant communities concentrate — South Indian restaurants, Malayalam signage, Filipino sari-sari stores, Bangladeshi groceries, the smell of cardamom and turmeric. Dubai Creek at sunset is where laborers gather on benches because it's free and outdoors and the breeze comes off the gulf. The cuisine, the texture, and the workers who built and run the city are almost entirely South and Southeast Asian, even though the public image is Emirati luxury. Behind every glass tower: prefab concrete dormitories painted beige, washing lines on every balcony, bunk beds inside.

## Odessa

**Working life and gentrified zone are the same because of the war.** Odessa is at war and still functioning. Russian drone strikes and missile attacks hit port infrastructure, power plants, residential buildings, and grain terminals continuously, but the city of one million people goes to school, opens cafes, hosts forums, runs its port (Ukraine's main maritime gateway), and continues to be itself. Schools have repaired and fortified basements; many dug deeper and raised ceilings. Hospitals run on generators when the grid goes. Most schools are back to in-person; some kindergartens remain hybrid. Mid-April 2026: a Russian drone attack killed twelve people including a child. Two days later: cafes were open. The cultural texture is post-Soviet Black Sea — Italian-influenced architecture from the 19th-century imperial period (Odessa was an open city of European immigration), Soviet apartment blocks, Jewish heritage partially rebuilt after near-destruction, Ukrainian and Russian languages spoken interchangeably with complex post-2022 identity politics around which language is "yours." Veterans are everywhere — wounded, working, organizing. Life under sustained war isn't dramatic moment by moment; it's bureaucratic. It's making sure the kindergarten has a generator and the basement is reinforced and the route to school doesn't pass under a known target. The most ordinary city you've ever seen, doing ordinary things, with the air-raid app on every phone, and a steady inflow of displaced people from Mariupol and Kherson trying to make new lives in shared apartments and overcrowded school districts.

## What transfers to LINE IN THE SAND

Two layers in each city. The first layer is the photogenic one — the one tourists see, magazines feature, cyberpunk and specialty-coffee aesthetics borrow from. The second layer is where most of the population lives and labors. THE LINE pulls from the second layer, with the first layer occasionally visible — the cracked-open view into a covenant facility cafe, the brief expensive trip up to the upper decks, the magazine in someone's hand showing a place they will never go.

**The mall in THE LINE is layered.** The upper levels pull from gentrified Riyadh — late-night patisseries, esports arenas, comedy clubs, family lounges, the warm-gold incandescent global-megamall lighting. The lower levels — where most people actually eat — pull from Singapore hawker centres, Hong Kong dai pai dongs, Baghdad's Karrada sidewalk-chair coffee culture, and Riyadh's Manfouha-style mixed-diaspora food street where Sudanese coffee neighbors Egyptian kebab neighbors Bangladeshi rice neighbors Filipino lumpia. The signage is in four scripts. Fluorescent above, gold below. Shared tables, fast turnover, the auntie remembers your order from yesterday.

**The safehouse is Sonapur and Singapore Westlite and Hong Kong subdivided flat compressed into one space.** A bunk in a row of bunks. Wire mesh or thin plywood between you and the next person. One bulb. A sink. The shared bathroom is down the corridor. Your belongings are in a duffel under the bed because there is no closet. Washing line strung across the room with someone else's underwear drying on it. The constant low hum of building ventilation. The smell of twelve different cooking traditions seeping through the walls because everyone is making dinner at the same time on hot plates. You don't have privacy. You have a bed.

**The scrap zone is Surat power-loom floor crossed with Sham Shui Po electronics scrap district crossed with Sadr City's improvised informal economy.** The defining sense is sound — deafening machine clatter that doesn't stop, headphones blasting Hindi pop or Cantopop or Arab rap to compete, the kind of sound that has destroyed 94% of workers' hearing by their forties. The defining smell is chemical — synthetic dust, hot copper, dye runoff, coolant, ozone from electrical arcs. The defining sight is fabric piled high enough to sleep on, electronics carcasses stripped for components, twelve people sharing one fluorescent-lit hall. The defining body is hands stained permanently with dye or solder flux, lungs full of silica, scars from machine accidents that healed without proper medical care. This isn't aesthetic decay. This is what active industrial labor in 2026 actually looks like and sounds like and smells like.

**The fence shop is the Karama back-of-shop, the Manfouha Sudanese electronics store, the Sham Shui Po electronics rebuild stall.** A small room with worn cloth and stained linoleum, a fluorescent flicker overhead, faded calligraphy on the walls in Arabic and Chinese, a man whose hands tell you he's been doing this for decades, electronics scrap sorted on a back table, the smell of solder, the smell of cardamom coffee from the next room. A radio playing in a language you almost understand. A photograph of a grandchild back home pinned above the till.

**The chengzhongcun layer — Beijing's urban villages — gives us the corridor texture between districts.** Dirt alleys where the official paved road ends. Self-built rental units 2-8 stories stacked against each other. Garbage piled in heaps because no one's collecting it. Washing lines crossing overhead like webbing. Children playing in narrow concrete gaps because the courtyards have been built over. The high-rise districts of the upper decks visible from the alley but unreachable without a permit. This is the back-of-the-mall, the corridor behind the storefront, the service lane.

**The covenant facility pulls from the formal Saudi state architecture and Chinese imperial precision** — cream and lacquer black and gold and mosque blue. Geometric calligraphy. Marble and glass. The 淼 chop on every door. The temperature is controlled. The lighting is even. The surfaces are maintained. It's the only place in THE LINE that doesn't feel improvised — and that's how you recognize it.

**The mosque, shrine, and small religious spaces are the Sadr City posters of Imam Hussein, the Najd Village mudbrick interiors, the Wudaoying Lama Temple, and the Shabandar Café's accumulated dignity.** The rare places where the lighting is soft and from everywhere, where someone has maintained the surfaces because they love them, where you can sit and be quiet without being charged for the chair.

**The exterior — the desert outside THE LINE — is the Empty Quarter, the Karoo, the Gobi, the salt flats around Karbala in late summer.** Mirrored wall reflecting deep purple-black sky. Dust at human height. Sodium streetlamps disappearing into vast distance. The few people out there are the ones who have nothing left to lose and the ones whose work is to make sure nothing gets in.

**The people in THE LINE are who they already are in 2026 — and that's the whole point.** They are the Sonapur crane operators, the Singapore Westlite construction workers, the Surat power-loom Odia migrants, the Baghdad Sadr City single mothers, the Sham Shui Po coffin-home night-shift security guards, the Beijing chengzhongcun food-delivery riders, the Manfouha Sudanese coffee-shop owners, the Odessa kindergarten teachers running classes between drone alerts. They are also the Riyadh esports players, the Baghdad streetwear designers, the Wudaoying baristas, the Karrada gallery curators, the Singapore hawker-stall third-generation cooks, the Dubai Mall personal shoppers. Both layers, in one population, four generations deep into the covenant.

The artist's job is to draw them as the people they already are. Not as future, not as exotic, not as cyberpunk archetype, not as gentrified-cafe minimal — as the working multipolar majority who are already running the world in 2026, whose grandchildren live in THE LINE because there was no other place to go.
